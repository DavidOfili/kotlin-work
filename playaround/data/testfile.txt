Exhortation
Christians sometimes use “open door” almost synonymously with “God’s will”: I prayed about it, then the opportunity appeared, therefore God opened the door. But David’s experience challenges that assumption powerfully. David had not simply developed a personal ambition to become king; God had already sent Samuel to anoint him (1 Samuel 16:1,12–13). Yet Saul remained on the throne, hunted David and stood between him and what God had already declared concerning his future. Then Saul unexpectedly entered the very cave where David and his men were hiding. David hadn’t arranged it, Saul was completely vulnerable, and David’s companions immediately interpreted the circumstances spiritually: “This is the day the LORD spoke of…” (1 Samuel 24:4). In today’s language we might have said, “Bro, how many confirmations do you need?” Yet David concluded that the opportunity was not permission. That gives us an important principle: an opportunity can be consistent with what you are expecting from God without being God’s way of bringing it about. An opportunity tells us what is possible; it does not necessarily tell us what God approves.

Remarkably, God allowed David to face a similar situation again. In 1 Samuel 26, David entered Saul’s camp and found Saul sleeping, with his spear beside him. This time Abishai supplied the interpretation: “Today God has delivered your enemy into your hands” (v.8), and even offered to do the killing himself. David still refused. His reasoning was not based merely on whether the circumstances looked miraculous or whether another believer interpreted them as God’s provision; he considered what he understood about his responsibility before God: “The LORD himself will strike him… But the LORD forbid that I should lay a hand on the LORD’s anointed” (vv.10–11). David could believe God had chosen him for the throne without believing God had authorised him to kill Saul to reach it. That distinction matters enormously. We can become so convinced about where God is taking us that we begin to justify whatever appears to move us there. But the destination can look right while the route is wrong.

Scripture also prevents us from becoming suspicious of every opportunity. In Acts 16:6–10, Paul intended to preach in Asia, but the Holy Spirit prevented the journey; he and his companions then tried to enter Bithynia, but the Spirit did not permit them. Eventually Paul received the Macedonian vision, and they concluded that God had called them to preach there. Later Paul could genuinely say, “A great door for effective work has opened to me” (1 Corinthians 16:9). But notice what immediately follows: “and there are many who oppose me.” So we should reject two convenient formulas: easy does not automatically mean God opened it, and difficult does not automatically mean God closed it. God’s opportunities can involve opposition, while apparently effortless opportunities can lead us somewhere God never asked us to go. Circumstances matter, but circumstances need interpretation; they cannot become our highest authority.

Jesus’ temptation makes the distinction even sharper. Satan showed Him the kingdoms of the world and offered them to Him (Matthew 4:8–10). Yet Jesus truly is King, and Psalm 2:8 speaks of the nations becoming the Son’s inheritance. The temptation therefore illustrates something deeper than simply being offered something bad: something consistent with God’s ultimate purpose was being offered through a route contrary to obedience to God. Jesus refused it. We can face smaller versions of the same test. We pray for financial provision and a lucrative opportunity appears—but requires dishonesty. We pray for promotion and suddenly discover an opportunity to undermine the colleague standing between us and the position. We pray for a relationship and someone apparently perfect appears—but pursuing it requires compromising what God has made clear. We pray for a business to succeed and a transformative contract appears—but securing it requires us to bend our convictions. We pray for relief and an escape route appears—but taking it would mean abandoning responsibilities God has entrusted to us. Something resembling the answer can sometimes become the test.

So how do we discern? Scripture gives us firmer ground than simply saying, “I prayed and felt peaceful about it.” First, God’s opportunity will not require us to disobey God’s revealed Word; God does not need our sin to accomplish His purposes. Second, distinguish God’s purpose from our preferred method; David could trust God’s destination without manufacturing the route. Third, treat circumstances as evidence to consider, not authority to obey; open doors, closed doors and other people’s interpretations must still be tested. Fourth, examine what accepting the opportunity requires us to become: does it require deception, vengeance, manipulation, neglect, pride or compromised responsibility? Finally, remain genuinely willing for God’s answer to be, “Not this way.” Discernment becomes especially difficult once we have already decided what God’s answer must look like. Last week we considered what David was becoming while he waited; today his story confronts us at the other end of waiting: when what you have been waiting for suddenly appears within reach, how do you know whether it is God’s opportunity—or simply an opportunity?
